Short tutorial and heads-up for defenders: `ntdll.dll` is no longer a safe anchor for detecting malicious behavior. Modern malware can sidestep it with direct syscalls.

## What changed
- Traditional EDR hooks instrument `ntdll` user-mode stubs to see system calls.
- Projects like SysWhispers, Hell's Gate, and sRDI let attackers generate their own syscall stubs or patch in fresh syscall numbers.
- Once a process swaps in custom stubs, your `ntdll` hooks never fire.

## How the bypass works (high level)
1. Malware maps or crafts syscall stubs at runtime (often from a clean syscall list).
2. It jumps straight into `syscall`/`sysenter` instructions, skipping `ntdll` entirely.
3. Any user-mode hooks on `ntdll` are bypassed, so telemetry and blocking logic miss the call.

## What to do instead
- Monitor at the kernel boundary (e.g., ETW/TiP events, kernel callbacks, or minifilters) where the syscall lands.
- Cross-check unexpected syscalls against process allowlists instead of trusting user-mode hooks.
- Treat any solution that only hooks `ntdll` as legacy; phase it out in favor of kernel or hardware-assisted visibility.

## Takeaway
If your detections depend on `ntdll` user-mode hooks, consider them deprecated. Malware can route around them with autogenerated syscall shims, so plan a migration to kernel-level or more resilient telemetry.
