**Lede:**  
The days when `ntdll.dll` stood as a reliable anchor for detecting malicious behavior are over. Advanced threats now bypass such hooks entirely by issuing direct syscalls — leaving many traditional defenses blind.

## What changed  
- Security tools historically relied on user-mode hooks inside `ntdll` to intercept system calls.  
- Toolkits like SysWhispers, Hell's Gate, and sRDI empower attackers to generate their own syscall stubs or patch in fresh syscall numbers.  
- Once a process swaps in custom syscall stubs, the original `ntdll`-based hooks never fire — effectively blinding the monitoring system.

## How the bypass works  
The bypass executes in three simple yet effective moves:  
1. The malware maps or crafts syscall stubs at runtime — often using a clean, trusted syscall list.  
2. It jumps directly into low-level `syscall` / `sysenter` instructions, skipping `ntdll` stubs entirely.  
3. As a result, user-mode hooks on `ntdll` are bypassed — telemetry and blocking logic fail to detect the call.

## What defenders should do instead  
- Shift monitoring to the kernel boundary: use kernel callbacks, ETW/TiP events, or minifilters — wherever the syscall actually lands.  
- Cross-check unexpected syscalls against a strict process allow-list instead of trusting user-mode hooks.  
- Treat any tool that only hooks `ntdll` as legacy and phase it out in favor of kernel- or hardware-assisted visibility.

## Final word  
If your security stack still relies on `ntdll` user-mode hooks for syscall monitoring, consider that approach deprecated. Modern malware can route around those defenses with autogenerated syscall stubs — leaving your telemetry blind. For real security, plan now: migrate to kernel-level or more resilient syscall detection before the next bypass becomes routine.
